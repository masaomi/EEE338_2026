What the MDS plot is, and how it differs from a PCA

edgeR's picture and a PCA, on the same six libraries · EEE338 2026 · 1,497 genes

Both do the same job: take six points in 1,497 dimensions and put them on a flat page. They go about it differently, and the difference is bigger than it looks.

1. PCA — find the direction the points spread along

Using all six points at once, find the line that the points spread furthest along when projected onto it. That is the first principal component. The second is the widest direction at right angles to it.

The dashed line is the search; the solid one is what it settles on, and is all that remains if you pause or print this. The points are the real six libraries — dark blue halleri, gold lyrata.

Every distance is measured with the same single ruler. Each axis is a weighted sum of all the genes, so you can ask afterwards which genes built this axis. That is a loading.

2. edgeR's MDS — a different ruler for every pair

This is the part worth knowing. plotMDS defaults to gene.selection = "pairwise" and top = 500, which means:

To measure the distance between samples A and B, use only the 500 genes with the largest log-fold-change between A and B. To measure A against C, choose a different 500, picked for that pair.

The genes change with the pair. By how much? Counted:

The two rows are the 500 genes of two different pairs. The dashed box is what they share. The red frame is the ruler itself, sliding across as the pair changes.

which is why it is not a PCA

At its worst, 178 of 500. The distance from A to B and the distance from A to C are measured with gene sets that disagree about more than half their members. The axes have no loadings: the question "which genes make up this axis" does not exist for this plot. The next section shows, measured, that the formula is the same as PCA's and why the loadings still do not come out of it.

The same formula, given something else to work on

The MDS step — double-centre the distance matrix, take its eigenvectors — is identical in both cases. And applied to Euclidean distances it is algebraically the same thing as PCA. PCA is the special case of MDS where the distances came from actual points.

Checked. The coordinates from gene.selection = "common" against a PCA of those same 500 genes:

dimension 1, |r| = 1.0000000000 dimension 2, |r| = 1.0000000000 scale (PCA / MDS) = 22.36068 = √500 ← the distance was divided by it

Every printed digit. Not a different method — the same one, reached another way.

The default, pairwise, hands that same formula a distance matrix that no arrangement of points produced. It was assembled pair by pair from different genes, so there is no original configuration for it to recover.

Which is why the axes have no loadings. And that is the same thing as saying this plot is not a linear transformation: there is no matrix that writes the coordinates as weighted sums of genes.

why it cannot be linear — there is a choosing step

Taking the top 500 is a selection, and its edge is a knife-edge. For the h1–l1 pair:

rank 500 0.616216 rank 501 0.612745 a gap of 0.003471 (0.56 %)

16 genes sit within 0.01 of that edge. Move the data a little and the membership swaps and the distance jumps. It is not even continuous, so it cannot be linear.

one thing I expected and did not find

A distance matrix that no points produced should show up as a negative eigenvalue. On these six libraries there is none (+1.556 +0.359 +0.250 +0.159 +0.150), so this particular matrix does happen to be embeddable in a Euclidean space. Nothing guarantees that in general, but the evidence for it is not here. Having no loadings and having coordinates at all are separate questions.

when they do come apart

When one library has a large problem in a handful of genes. A per-pair ruler picks those genes up only in the pairs that library is in, so MDS is the better tool for spotting one bad sample, and PCA is the better tool when you want to ask what the axis means. Different jobs; neither is the correct one.

5. The plot is a picture of the design

All three put halleri against lyrata on the horizontal and plant on the vertical. That is not a coincidence.

horizontal 61.8 % ← the contrast being tested today (subgenome) vertical 15.0 % ← the factor being blocked on today (plant)

The largest source of variation is the question you are asking, and the second largest is the plant. Which is exactly why removing the plant with ~ plant + subgenome makes the test stronger — and it was visible in this picture before any test was run.

Turn it round: if the horizontal axis were neither the treatment nor the plant, that is where to stop and find out what it is. Drawing the MDS first is for that.

6. For reference — t-SNE and UMAP (non-linear)

All three so far are on the same side of one line: however they go about it, they are trying to reproduce the distances they were given, faithfully and everywhere. t-SNE and UMAP take a different bargain — keep near things near, and throw the far distances away.

That is a separate axis from linear-or-not. As section 2 showed, the default MDS is already not linear — it has no loadings — and it still tries to keep the distances. The two questions are independent.

A sketch, not a measurement. One dataset drawn twice. The groups hold; their positions, sizes and orientations do not.

three things not to read off one
  1. The distance between clusters. Far apart on the page does not mean far apart in the data
  2. The size of a cluster. The method evens densities out, so a spread is not the spread it came from
  3. Reproducibility. A different random seed is a different picture, and so is a different perplexity or n_neighbors

And they are no use on six samples. These are tools for the local structure of thousands to tens of thousands of points, which is why they are standard in single-cell work. Point one at a handful of bulk libraries and it will return a picture — the danger is that it returns one at all.
