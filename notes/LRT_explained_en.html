What a likelihood ratio test actually does

One gene, six numbers, worked all the way through · EEE338 2026 · HMA4 (AT2G19110)

The lecture says: fit two models, compare their likelihoods, turn the difference into a p-value. This opens that sentence out, on the real numbers from the matrix you build in the practical.

1. One gene, six numbers

ALK root, zinc treated, three plants. Each plant carries both subgenomes, so one gene gives six numbers.

librarycount
halleri rep1 / rep2 / rep3548 / 378 / 476
lyrata rep1 / rep2 / rep349 / 55 / 60

There is one question.

Can these six numbers be said to come from one mean, or do they need two?

2. A likelihood is the probability of the whole dataset

Counts are treated as negative binomial. Fix a mean μ and a dispersion φ, and you can work out the probability of seeing exactly 548.

The libraries are independent, so the probability of seeing all six is the product of the six probabilities.

L = P(548) × P(378) × P(476) × P(49) × P(55) × P(60) log L = log P(548) + log P(378) + … + log P(60)

Taking logs turns the product into a sum. You add because you are multiplying — not because you are averaging. An average would throw away the fact that there were six libraries at all.

3. Two models. Only the mean differs

This gene's dispersion φ is the same in the null model and in the alternative. Between the two models, only the mean moves.

Left: the null model, explaining all six with the single mean 261. Right: the alternative, 467 for halleri and 55 for lyrata. The dashed lines are those means.

the word "model" does two jobs

NB(467, φ) and NB(55, φ) are two different distributions — same φ, different mean. A hypothesis, on the other hand, is the whole specification of which distribution each library gets. The likelihood belongs to the hypothesis, so there is only ever one of it, and no step where two likelihoods are combined.

4. How far each of the six terms lifts

Both models add up six terms. The only difference is which mean went into each one.

Red on the left is the null, green on the right the alternative. The length of each line is that library's improvement.

The null model has to explain 548 and 49 with the same mean 261, so both come out unlikely (-19.2 and -31.7). Give it two means and every library sits near one of them, so all six terms lift at once.

5. D, and the p-value

# twice the difference of the log-likelihoods D = 2 × ( -27.24 − (-128.93) ) = 203.4 # the test's df = how many parameters the alternative has spare null has 1 mean, alternative has 2. It gained 1 → test df = 1 # upper tail of chi-square with 1 degree of freedom p = 3.8e-46

Why the count of spare parameters is the df

Give a model one extra parameter and it fits a little better even when the null is true, because it can chase the noise. D goes up for free.

By how much, for free? About 1 per extra parameter. And the mean of a chi-square with k degrees of freedom is exactly k.

Checked. Draw six counts from one negative binomial — so the null really is true — fit one mean and then two, and form D. 40,000 times.

D, drawnchi-square(1), in theory
mean0.9991

They agree. That agreement is what "one extra parameter = one degree of freedom" means. And of those 40,000 draws, the number that reached HMA4's D = 203.4 was 0.

why 1 and not 2

Because φ is shared between the two models. Estimate a separate φ per group and the alternative gains two parameters, not one, and the test's df would be 2. edgeR borrows φ across all genes and fixes it before fitting the means — not for convenience, but to keep the test this shape.

6. That was the simplified version

Three things above were made easier than the real thing.

  1. Library sizes were ignored. The real fit scales each mean by how deep that library was sequenced
  2. The dispersion was held at one common value, φ = 0.02274, the square of the measured common BCV of 0.1508. The real fit uses a per-gene value
  3. Plant-to-plant differences were ignored. The practical's design is ~ plant + subgenome, which removes them first

7. And the practical does not run a likelihood ratio test

The script says:

fit <- glmQLFit(d, design, robust = TRUE) qlf <- glmQLFTest(fit, coef = "subgenomehalleri")

glmFit + glmLRT would be the likelihood ratio test, against a chi-square. glmQLFit + glmQLFTest is a quasi-likelihood F-test.

One thing separates them: whether φ is treated as known, or as the estimate it actually is.

Same data, same design, same gene:

teststatisticp-value
plain likelihood ratio test (chi-square)LR = 718.43 2.9 × 10-158
quasi-likelihood F-test — what the practical runs F = 673.891.7 × 10-46

A ruler of p-values; the ticks are powers of ten. The same six numbers move 112 orders of magnitude on the choice of test alone. That the hand calculation lands next to the quasi-likelihood mark is a coincidence — the roughness of the simplified version happened to pull the same way. What it computes is the plain likelihood ratio test, the same thing as the red mark at the far right.

How that F was put together

One division.

# the LR on the same fit, over the test's df, over this gene's quasi-dispersion F = 716.425 / 1 / 1.06312 = 673.89 # upper tail of the F distribution. Look at the denominator df p = upper tail of F(673.89,  1,  101.0277) = 1.7e-46

The hard part is not the F. It is the 1.06312 it was divided by.

From this gene alone that number came out at 2.62392. But of the six counts, very little is left over to measure scatter with once four means have been fitted — edgeR counts it as 2. A number built on that cannot be trusted, so it is pulled back towards 1.0316, the value all the genes together give.

The pull. What this gene brings of its own weighs 2; the other 4,288 genes weigh 99.0279. So what gets used stays close to the left.

( 2 × 2.62392 + 99.0279 × 1.0316 ) / ( 2 + 99.0279 ) = 1.06312

And 2 + 99.0279 = 101.0277 is the denominator df of the F above. That number is the record of how much went into knowing σ². A chi-square has no such denominator.

φ again — this time from gene to gene

As in section 3, φ does not move between the null model and the alternative; that is true of both tests. What differs is whether φ changes when you move from one gene to the next.

fitNB φ usedper gene?
glmFit → glmLRT 0.0015006yes, tagwise
glmQLFit → glmQLFTest 0.00151825no — one value for all 4,289
# likelihood ratio test — every gene difference lives in φ_g, and is believed Var = μ + φ_g μ² # quasi-likelihood F — φ shared; the difference moves to σ²_g, is squeezed, # and its uncertainty stays in the denominator df Var = σ²_g × ( μ + φ μ² )

It is not that one carries more variance than the other. They keep it in different places — and moving it is what lets the test carry how well that value is known.

this is the part worth keeping

What this gene knows about its own scatter is almost nothing. Believe it exactly and the tail runs out to 10-158. That number claims more than the data can support. The quasi-likelihood F-test folds the uncertainty of that estimate back into the denominator. It is more conservative, and that is why its FDR means something.
